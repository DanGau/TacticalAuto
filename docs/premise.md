# Premise

**Genre.** Isometric superhero auto battler, against an alien invasion. The player owns a small team of heroes who fight on their own, kicking down the door, and builds each one up over a run.

**Story.** Aliens have landed at the South Pole; their hub lies under the Antarctic ice. The player gathers an elite party of the world's last heroes, crossing the globe continent by continent to reach each one, then raids the hub. The aliens hunt the party harder with every stop it makes. A brief story opens each run, before its first choice.

**Theme.** Superheroes on Dungeons & Dragons rules, against an alien invasion. Powers over military tech: a sorcerer fights beside a tech genius, though it is not full fantasy, and the aliens stay aliens. The game speaks of heroes, a party, allies, HP and powers. Every power reads as a usage line (Passive, At will, Reaction, Aura or N/battle), range, target and at most two lines of effect (`powers.ts`). A hero reads as a character sheet: name, class and level, HP, armor, move, damage, range, crit, weapon and kit, powers (`ui/sheet.ts`); an alien as a stat block, on the deploy screen when tapped and on first meeting.

**Loop.** Roguelike. A won run saves the world; then more aliens arrive and the next run begins.

**Battle.** A town somewhere in the world, 96 tiles square, many screens across. The camera frames what is in play and follows the action, a drag pans it, and it shows past the map only to keep the party in frame. The fight is staged in an arena within it, about 30 tiles across: the aliens hang out there, objects stand between them in open ground, and the party never wanders the map to find them. The player's decision before a battle is which boon to take in. Each battle has one entry point, chosen by the encounter, where the party lands plainly. Nothing strikes the whole battlefield. Heroes never run: every fight is the party against the aliens near it. The battle then plays out in alternating turns, humans first. In a turn every unit of the side moves at once, then each attacks, one after another: a hero makes two attacks, its weapon's and its bonus attack, an alien one. A hero may keep some of its move to walk to a shot between its attacks, and to step back after them. A hero still uses at most one bigger ability a turn. Unit AI is predictable, so the player can foresee what a boon will do.

**Challenges.** Each battle tests one thing, readable at a glance on the Flight map and the deploy screen (`challenges.ts`), and a boon answers it: Slog (a swamp of mire with small groups scattered across it: levitation, swiftness, ranged shield), Firing line (long-range shooters on a ridge: ranged shield, obscuring mist), Iron wall (heavily armored aliens: sunder, overcharge), Swarm (bodies flood in: melee shield, rigged tank), Scattered (sentries spread out on a clock, then reinforcements: swiftness), Big one (a huge armored boss with an escort: overcharge, sunder), Kill box (a packed cluster at the way in: blinding flash, obscuring mist, melee shield). Tiers by party size draw them, rising up a leg. The first leg has none: its battles are normal fights, the first five stops all battles, so heroes build a kit before the first test. Meeting one without its answer costs dear in health and may lose the fight; health carries over, so repeated mismatches end the run. Extractions and the hub are Big ones.

**Encounters.** Each battle is designed as one set-piece, as a DM prepares an encounter (`encounter.ts`): a fight flows along an axis from the way in to an overlook the aliens hold. The arena is open ground, buildings and clutter cleared; fuel tanks stand in the open; heaps of things to hurl lie by the flanks; a ledge rises by a flank. Aliens gather in clusters of varied size and shape (`formations.ts`): pairs, trios, loose crowds, a line across a street, a bow, a ring around something, a file along a road, one big alien with small ones pressed about it, the odd lone sentry. The ground shapes them: on a height, along a wall, about an object, on a road, and a few stand by what the mission is about (the uplink, the terror's heart, the VIP, the commander on the overlook). Reinforcements arrive at set gates behind and beside the aliens.

**Objects.** A hero uses up to three objects a battle in place of an attack: it hurls what stands beside it at an alien, or sets off a fuel tank beside the aliens. Heroes value a tile beside something to hurl.

**Engaging.** Aliens stand in clusters; none patrols. The deploy screen shows every alien on the map, but burrowers, which stay hidden underground. Each battle is plainly one of two flows, by mission and challenge. A push (Clear them out, Takedown, Bring them back, VIP rescue, raids): clusters stand apart along the fight's axis; the one nearest the way in engages, and each other holds its post and fire until a hero comes within 5 tiles or one of it is hurt, then engages whole, so the party knocks them down one at a time, with several on a hero at once. If nobody is fighting for two rounds, or six pass in one fight, the nearest cluster comes on its own. A flood (Hold, Hunted, Rescue, a Swarm): a third of the force is posted and fights at once; the rest pours in as clumps of two to five every second round, at a gate clear of the party. Once the party is in, fog darkens every tile no hero sees. The party never hides: every turn is a full turn, and heroes march on the fight at their full move from the first. The party moves as one: every hero closes on the same enemy, the one nearest the party's centre, and stays near that centre. Heroes never take cover or weigh what could shoot them: a hero with a shot takes the tile with the best shot, counting height and something to hurl. A hero with no shot dashes on to one if it can, else takes a standard action in place of attacking: with an alien able to reach it next turn it prepares, overwatching to shoot the first alien to come in range; otherwise it sprints, moving again toward the fight or the way out. While any alien is in sight, heroes avoid stepping where they would draw in an alien waiting its turn, so one cluster falls before the next engages. A hero walled in punches through the walls about it. On the march, Deadeyes keep behind the rest of the party.

**Power curve.** An alien kind's health never changes, so the aliens that are frightening to one hero are mown down by four built-up ones. The aliens come in numbers: a fight going the party's way looks the same, but one slipping turns deadly fast. Difficulty rises in ways the player can see: the alien force grows with each leg, new kinds appear, and deeper aliens are better equipped, with a point of armor for each step along the road and a point of damage for every third.

**Health and armor.** Every unit shows its health and armor as pips, one each, ten to a row: health in red, health lost in dark, armor in yellow. Armor is temporary health: it takes damage before health does, and is whole again at the start of each fight. It reduces no hit.

**Aliens.** Aliens win by rules no hero has, after XCOM's aliens and StarCraft's Zerg. A mission has a force to spend on themed handfuls of aliens: four swarmlings, two kinhounds with a trooper, a psion with a vise and an aegis, and so on; deeper along the road, more appear. Each then stands alone. As in D&D and superhero films, most aliens fight up close and a few shooters are a nuisance behind them; only a Slog and a Firing line are mostly shooters. Each brawler has one rule: a leaper pounces once over the front onto the hero who shoots farthest, a vise holds the hero it hits through their next turn, a dreadhorn rams harder after a run-up and drives a hero two tiles, an aegis halves every hit but one from beside it, a kinhound hits harder for each alien beside its prey, and a zealot hits harder as it is hurt. Deeper along the road come aliens that each punish a party lacking one answer and fold to a party that has it: a juggernaut's plates to many hits or shred, an incubator to burst before it hatches, a wraith to crits, burrowers that surface beside the loneliest hero to overwatch, a feeder that grows on every trick to plain shooting, a regenerator to fire, sprinters to chill, a hive node to reach, and a mirror, which hits as hard as the party's hardest hitter, to a party whose strength is spread. An Emplacement is a turret, an automated gun that never moves and shoots any hero in its sights, with a vise beside it. The first time the player meets one, the battle pauses on a full screen of it, its name and one calm line on what it will do, until the player acknowledges it. The flight map tells what each stop tests, and how many aliens of each danger, small, medium or large, it holds; a Seer's Uplink relic names them. Before the landing, the player is shown every alien where it stands, and what each kind does.

- Grunt: shoots from range, picking its ground.
- Swarmling: weak, fast, attacks only up close, and comes in numbers.
- Spitter: its spit leaves acid that burns whoever is still in it after their side moves.
- Burster: runs at the party and explodes, beside a hero or wherever it dies.
- Brute: tough, attacks only up close, and charges whoever hits it.
- Psion: panics a hero, who loses a turn of action; its death hurts the aliens near it.
- Harvester: on rescues; fast, attacks only up close, and a civilian it kills rises next turn as a husk, a weak alien that attacks up close.
- Boss: guards an extraction or the hub. Each hero already extracted makes the next boss tougher and adds a trooper to its escort; from the second extraction on it spawns swarmlings.

**Attacks.** Every attack hits, and every attack that deals damage deals at least 1, so damage is steady and the party wears down battle by battle. What changes it is height and skills; crits come at their stated chance. Tall objects and walls block shots; nothing shoots through a wall. An alien moves to where its best shot most outweighs the best shot coming back, bolder at full health and more careful as it is wounded. A badly wounded hero fights from out of arm's reach while a healthier ally fights on.

**Maps.** Each battlefield is built from objects, after XCOM 2's plots and parcels: roads cut it into lots, and each lot is a place, such as a house with rooms and furniture, a park of trees, a car park, a fenced depot of crates, a warehouse, or an open field. Vehicles stand on the roads. A zone on the road names the kind of battlefield its battles are fought in: town, city, countryside, industrial yard, or ruins. Among their lots are squares with a monument, markets, orchards with lanes between the trees, graveyards, and buildings fallen in. A satellite stands in a walled compound with a gate on each side. The alien source is a hive of spires and growths with nothing human in it. Walls and windows stand on the lines between tiles, with doorways through them; a hero shoots through a window and not through a wall. Trees, trucks and stacks are tall, blocking movement and shots; cars, crates, fences and windows are low, blocking movement alone. A blast wrecks the objects and walls it catches, opening new lines of fire. Roofs within the arena's circle, and over any unit in view, are cut away, so what stands inside shows; the rest stay on. Post-apocalyptic cities and countryside are the setting.

**Run.** A run begins by picking the hero who leads the party, a small globe turning to the city they wait in. A run is three legs, each crossing a continent to a city, the first two where an elite hero holds out: the leg ends in an extraction, a guarded fight that adds the hero to the party, seasoned. Then a last leg leads to the alien hub; winning that final mission saves the world. The map, Flight, is a globe filling the screen under the header, with no instructions, framed on where the party stands and the stops ahead, far enough in that no two crowd, and turned so the way on leads up: each stop a step along the way across the continent, always at a real town, the steps uneven and the trails never crossing; the journey so far in gold, trails dotted from where the party stands to each choice, the choices pulsing. One tap on a stop picks it and lights the way on from it; a second flies the party's helicopter there, and into a fight dives down through the clouds. The globe glides from view to view, the choices fading in as it settles. A battle's town is built the way its continent builds.

**Road.** A leg is a map of trails that part and meet, nine stops long whichever way is taken, then its extraction. Each stop leads to one, two or three in the next column, and the player picks among them. It shows the stop the party stands on and the stops still in reach up to three ahead; stops behind and stops beyond sight stay hidden. A leg's first stops are battles; the rest are drawn at random: a battle, a shop, an unknown stop, a medic, or a supply drop or gear cache, which need no fight. At a medic (✚) the party rests and heals fully; a party already at full health instead takes a piece of rare gear or a level for its lowest hero. Each battle has its own risk, goal and kind of battlefield: a quiet one has fewer aliens and pays no gear; a contested one is a fair fight with an even chance of a piece of gear; an overrun one has more aliens and pays a piece of rare gear. So a choice weighs different journeys, not easy against hard. An extraction and the alien hub are each guarded by a boss.

**Missions.** Each battle has a goal, and each goal pays its own reward, so the road chosen steers the build. Clear them out: kill every alien, for supplies and gear. Hold and assassinate missions appear from the second leg, so a lone first hero never meets them. Takedown: kill a commander and the rest flee, for rare gear; it is armored, comes with two guards, three deeper along the road, survives any one shot from full health, and blinks to safety once brought to half. Hold the uplink: the uplink stands at the arena's heart and the entry point by it; end six rounds with a hero in its zone while clumps of aliens pour in every second round, at a gate clear of the party; then an extraction zone appears in the edge, and they keep coming until every hero left has lifted off from it, for a relic; aliens take the uplink, then go for the party. Rescue: a terror site. Ten civilians are scattered about the terror's heart, and the party bursts in among them. The aliens come for the party, but an alien with no good shot at a hero shoots a civilian instead, a civilian's shot counting for less than a hero's. So a party that hangs back loses civilians, and one that pushes in, taking the risk, saves more. Civilians flee the aliens toward the party, and the party, never cautious here, moves as one on the alien nearest a civilian, or with none near one, the nearest civilian, a hero taking a chance to reach a civilian over a shot; a hero, or a mech it called down, beside a civilian gets them out. Saving more than half wins it; saving half or fewer loses it, and it pays nothing. Won, each saved pays supplies and every hero standing experience, and every four left behind add a threat; it ends once none is left. VIP rescue, from the second leg: a VIP is held in a building within, two aliens standing guard. The first hero within 3 tiles blows in the wall between them, catching the guards so they do not shoot on their next turn; a hero within 3 tiles frees them once no alien in the fight that can move stands within 10 tiles. Then an extraction zone appears in the edge, a walk of about 24 tiles away. The VIP follows the party there, keeping within 2 tiles of a hero, while the party keeps by the VIP; nothing hurts a held VIP, but the aliens shoot a freed one before anyone. The VIP lifts off at the zone, handed on by any hero there, and that wins it; losing the VIP loses the mission. Won, it pays supplies and a relic. Smash and grab: three guarded caches; each grabbed pays supplies. Bring them back: never drawn; it comes when the aliens hold heroes (see Death). Hovering a battle on the map counts its aliens by kind.

**Threat.** One bar for the whole invasion, and the run's main race. Every stop adds two, and a lost battle more, so a longer road costs more. The hunt bites as the bar fills: every battle has more aliens, up to half again at full, and the map's force for a stop counts them. When it is full, the party is ambushed on the road before the next stop: survive the ambush, which empties the bar if won. Unpaid down, it fills about once a leg. As in War of the Chosen, the party is hunted: there is no landing and no planning; the game cuts straight to the party in the arena, the Hunter engaged first, a mini-boss about half again as strong as the party's strongest hero. The party stands and fights to the last alien, and almost always ends it bruised. Ambushes are meant to be the main way a run is lost.

**Start.** A title screen leads to the run's first choice, as Slay the Spire's opening blessing: three recruits of different classes, each card a compact character sheet. Every hero starts with a random class and a random common weapon, melee included.

**Overworld.** Between stops the player moves among three views: the Map, which shows the whole run as trails of stops and the ways on; the Team, which lists the party's keywords, oaths, boons and relics; and the Heroes, which shows each hero's character sheet and feats. After a stop come the choices it left, one screen each: a relic, each piece of gear found, each skill owed. A skill screen names the hero and its options, and nothing of what lies after. Then one debrief reveals the threat and supplies, and each hero by class badge, name and weapon, its experience bar filling and flaring on a level gained, and feats it pulled off; beneath them a crate for each find. The player then returns to the Map.

**Losing.** A run is lost only in a battle, never on the map, and suddenly rather than slowly. Losing an ambush, an extraction, a recovery or the final mission ends the run, as does a wipe. Losing any other battle adds threat and earns nothing. Health carries from battle to battle, as in Slay the Spire: each stop travelled to heals every hero 2, and a hero downed comes back at 1. So a strong party rampages, and one bad fight sends it the safe way, toward a medic. The Heroes view, the debrief and the battle show each hero's health and armor.

**Progression.**

- Shops: won battles, supply drops and sold gear pay supplies, spent only at shops on the road: five weapons with an effect, each bought for the hero the player picks; four relics; a boon; and a bribe that lowers the threat. A party can never buy one out. Each shop rolls its own stock, and no two pieces of gear in it share an effect. What is bought stays in its place, greyed out and marked Purchased, so the shop never shifts under the player's hand.
- Boons: the run starts with a pick of two of three commons; the party holds up to three, kept for the run and never used up. Each is common, rare or epic: a combo, rare or epic, does one effect in full and one or two others at less. More are rare finds, the commoner the likelier: from won battles, an extraction, an ambush and shops; finding one with three held forces a choice of which to drop. On the deploy screen the player reads the map and the way in and picks one for that fight, or with none held taps to start; an ambush has no entry point and so no boon. They are swiftness (more move), levitation (no extra cost on steep or difficult ground, over chasms and down drops, and no fall when shoved), overcharge (more damage), sunder (attacks ignore armor), a rigged tank (the player picks a fuel tank; it blows once aliens crowd it, a hero close by caught too), obscuring mist (for three rounds no alien shoots beyond two tiles), a blinding flash (the first cluster stunned on entry), a ranged shield and a melee shield (attacks of that kind do half damage).
- Unknown stops: as Slay the Spire's question marks, revealed on arrival as a battle, a shop, or an event met once a run. An event offers options that each help and hurt. Some swear an oath (`oaths.ts`) that changes the rules for the rest of the run: Mass Cloning (clones fill the party, less health), Lone Wolf (one hero, much stronger, no recruits), Specialists (every hero a different class), Power Armor (armor, for move), Iron Skin (every hit on either side does less), Brawlers (damage for range). Fighting oaths change how the whole party fights, and the party fights one way at a time: Blademasters (melee only, charging in, blunting shots from range), Psi Awakened (attacks through walls, ignoring armor), Wreckers (each hit wrecks the objects around its target), Gunslingers (a third shot a turn). Others trade supplies, threat, heroes and relics.
- Party: the run begins with one hero. The first two extractions won each add a hero, to three, the most a party has; the third storms a stronghold for a relic and a boon, and a hero joins at level 2, its skills learned, met on a screen of its own before the debrief. In the debrief each hero owed a skill has a button to choose it, and each find is a crate to open; both open in a pop-up over the debrief, which stays still behind, and the crate asks which hero takes the find, showing what each would replace and how its stats would change, or sells it. Every hero who fought gains experience, downed or not: a little shared for fighting and winning, the rest earned by what each did, its kills and damage done, so a hero who carries a fight snowballs. A feat, such as killing an alien at full health with one hit, pays a little more the first time a hero pulls it off, so later recruits can catch up by doing what the veteran already has. Every hero has one of eight classes, drawn at random: the Vanguard, Powerhouse, Deadeye and Beacon; the Gadgeteer, who deploys a combat mech once aliens are in sight, or a swarm of light drones with Drone Swarm; the Pyro, whose Fireball sets a knot of aliens and their ground alight; the Cryo, whose every hit chills; and the Phantom, a superhero who cloaks when aliens have it in their sights: cloaked, no alien sees or targets it, and its next attack strikes out of nowhere, harder and past armor, and reveals it. The cloak is a power, not sneaking: the Phantom fights every turn like any hero, and shimmers, faint, on the player's screen. The Pyro and Cryo start a burn or frost build. A mech is a frail decoy, 3 health, that every alien able to shoot it must shoot; it fights for the party, and its kills are its engineer's, but it cannot hold the field alone. Drones, 2 health each, are free for the aliens to ignore. A class is a set of stat changes; a way of positioning, such as a Deadeye hanging back, which its weapon may override, though a Beacon always keeps by the party; and one ability, which works with any weapon, the hero uses unprompted. An ability either replaces the hero's shot, as a Powerhouse's Meteor Strike does, or comes on top of it, as a Beacon's Healing Light does.
- Levels and powers: levels never stop; each takes more experience than the last. Each class has a tree of 17 to 20 skills, each a guard skill that keeps the party alive (shrugging off damage, absorbing a hit, armor, decoys, healing), a control skill that holds or moves the aliens (roots, stuns, knockbacks, throws, walls of fire), or a power skill that hits harder. A recruit arrives knowing its class power, the tree's root, which forks into three paths that fork again, each ending in a capstone; about half the forks hold twins, opposite takes on one idea, and taking one locks the other. At levels 3, 5, 7 and 11 a hero is offered 3 or 4 skills its tree has opened, learns one and loses the rest for the run; four picks reach one capstone, and a hero takes only one. Later levels add only stats. So two heroes of a class differ from run to run. Many skills are a hero's: a Vanguard leaps into a knot of aliens and throws them, a Powerhouse slams the ground or roars them into a stupor, a Gadgeteer pulses an EMP, a Beacon blinds them with holy light. Skills borrow from tabletop role-playing: a reaction shot on the enemy's turn, bonus damage on an exposed target, auras that help allies nearby, marking a target, getting a fallen ally up, a second action once a battle, forcing the enemy to target the tank. Whenever a power, a piece of gear or a relic shapes a shot or acts on a unit, its name shows over that unit, so the player sees each one at work.
- Relics: winning an extraction, a hold, a VIP rescue, an ambush or an Overrun battle offers three relics, and the party keeps one for the run. A relic changes every hero or how the run pays, as Slay the Spire's do, so each run is built around what it found. Many play on a mechanic: landing relics catch the nearest aliens surprised, blast, strip or chill them where the party comes in, raise barricades or bring a turret, so they make the way in matter more; Shredder and Cryo Charges are offered only once the party holds Breaching Charges. Others serve rescues, holds, ambushes and bosses.
- Keywords: Burn, Frost, Mark, Crit, Kill and Ward tie skills, gear and relics together, as a deckbuilder's card types do. Some pieces cause a keyword, such as gear that chills or a skill that marks; others pay off on it, such as a relic that adds damage to chilled targets. Two keywords can meet: a crit that sets a target burning, a hit on a target both burning and chilled. Cards colour each keyword in their text, lit where the party already has one, and define it in a box on hover, and the first relic offered builds on one the party has.
- Resists: some aliens shrug off a keyword, so no build is safe everywhere. Bursters cannot burn, brutes cannot be chilled, psions cannot be marked, and spitters' acid ignores armor. Each leg's boss resists one keyword, shown from the start of the leg so the party can build to beat it. 
- One entry point per mission, chosen by the encounter for its flow: a push's head on down the axis, never near an assassination's commander; a flood's by the uplink or among the civilians. The deploy screen shows the whole encounter, its alien kinds counted, and the way in; the player picks a boon or taps to start. An ambush has no entry point.
- Map layouts: each battlefield's plot lies over a shape that decides where the fighting funnels: an open grid of streets; a river crossed only at bridges; a coast that walls one side with water; a blast crater, open and coverless in the middle; or long walls that cut three lanes joined at a few gaps. Water blocks standing but not shots.
- Mission AI: the party fights to its goal. On a hold it heads for the uplink and stands its ground within 3 tiles of it, and the aliens converge there; on an assassination it closes on the commander once sighted; on a rescue it pushes in, careless of risk, on the alien nearest a civilian; on a VIP rescue it makes for the VIP, then escorts it out. A battle's alien force is its challenge's, by party size and rung, and varies by one either way.
- High ground: hills rise a level, their crests two. A unit sees 2 tiles farther for each level it stands above what it looks at, and 2 less for each below; a shot does 1 more damage per level downhill and 1 less uphill; and a step up a level costs 2 extra move, so charging a hill is slow. Raised tiles are drawn higher and lighter.
- Gear: gear is rare. A won battle drops at most one piece, by its goal and risk; a won extraction and a cache drop one. It goes to the party, not to any one hero. Each piece is a weapon built from parts, as Borderlands builds guns: the weapon itself, a stat mod, some of which trade one stat for another, or on rare and epic gear one effect, such as setting targets burning. A hero holds one item, its weapon; aliens keep their armor, and a hero's armor comes only from skills, relics and oaths. Found gear is decided at once: the player hands it to any hero, replacing and selling what that hero held, or sells it. Nothing is taken off or stored. Gear survives a death.
- Weapons: a weapon's base is the weapon itself, and any class wields any weapon. Weapons carry a superhero's or a myth's names: the arc caster, star lance that hangs back and strikes from afar, blast gauntlet that closes in, storm darts, storm engine that suppresses, twin blasters, and melee weapons that reach only the next tile and charge in: a thunder hammer that knocks aliens back, a sunblade that cleaves, a star shield that adds armor and blunts attacks from range, and power fists. A weapon's way of positioning, where it has one, overrides its wielder's class's.
- Death: nobody is left behind, and nobody is replaced. A hero brought to nothing is downed and lies where it fell. A party that wins with a hero standing on the field carries every hero home. A wipe, every hero in a battle downed and none lifted off, is game over, even if the last alien fell too. Getting out of a hold, a hero with its hands free goes for the nearest ally down, picks them up and carries them out, walking at half its move; a carrier who falls drops them. Heroes left downed are taken, and the next stop is a recovery, before the map goes on: the aliens hold them beside their posts, at a third of the leg's force, while all of them come again, a few every second round. A hero who reaches a captive gets them up at half health to fight. The recovery is all or nothing: win, and every captive rejoins; lose, and the run ends.
