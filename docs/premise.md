# Premise

**Genre.** Isometric superhero auto battler, against an alien invasion. The player picks one hero, who fights on their own, kicking down the door, and builds them up over a run. One hero, so the player always knows who is doing what, and each hero has room for depth.

**Story.** Aliens have landed at the South Pole; their hub lies under the Antarctic ice. The player's hero crosses the globe continent by continent, taking a satellite on each for its access key, then raids the hub. The aliens hunt the hero harder with every stop it makes. A brief story opens each run, before its first choice.

**Theme.** Superheroes on Dungeons & Dragons rules, against an alien invasion. Powers over military tech: a sorcerer fights beside a tech genius, though it is not full fantasy, and the aliens stay aliens. The game speaks of the hero, allies (a mech, drones, a turret), HP and powers. Every power reads as a usage line (Passive, At will, Reaction, Aura or N/battle), range, target and at most two lines of effect (`powers.ts`). A hero reads as a character sheet: name, class and level, HP, armor, move, damage, range, crit, weapon and kit, powers (`ui/sheet.ts`); an alien as a stat block, on the deploy screen when tapped and on first meeting.

**Loop.** Roguelike. A won run saves the world; then more aliens arrive and the next run begins.

**Battle.** A town somewhere in the world, 96 tiles square, many screens across. The camera frames what is in play and follows the action, a drag pans it, and it shows past the map only to keep the hero in frame. The fight is staged in an arena within it, about 30 tiles across: the aliens hang out there, objects stand between them in open ground, and the hero never wanders the map to find them. The player's decision before a battle is which boon to take in. Each battle has one entry point, chosen by the encounter, where the hero lands plainly. Nothing strikes the whole battlefield. The hero never runs: every fight is the hero against the aliens near it. The battle then plays out in alternating turns, humans first. In a turn every unit of the side moves at once, then each attacks, one after another: the hero makes two attacks, its weapon's and its bonus attack, an alien one. The hero may keep some of its move to walk to a shot between its attacks, and to step back after them. It still uses at most one bigger ability a turn. Unit AI is predictable, so the player can foresee what a boon will do.

**Challenges.** Each battle tests one thing, readable at a glance on the Flight map and the deploy screen (`challenges.ts`), and a boon answers it: Slog (a swamp of mire with small groups scattered across it: levitation, swiftness, ranged shield), Firing line (long-range shooters on a ridge: ranged shield, obscuring mist), Iron wall (heavily armored aliens: sunder, overcharge), Swarm (bodies flood in: melee shield, rigged tank), Scattered (sentries spread out on a clock, then reinforcements: swiftness), Big one (a huge armored boss with an escort: overcharge, sunder), Kill box (a packed cluster at the way in: blinding flash, obscuring mist, melee shield). Tiers by leg draw them, rising up a leg. The first leg has none: its battles are normal fights, the first five stops all battles, so the hero builds a kit before the first test. Meeting one without its answer costs dear in health and may lose the fight; health carries over, so repeated mismatches end the run. Extractions and the hub are Big ones.

**Encounters.** Each battle is designed as one set-piece, as a DM prepares an encounter (`encounter.ts`): a fight flows along an axis from the way in to an overlook the aliens hold. The arena is open ground, buildings and clutter cleared; fuel tanks stand in the open; heaps of things to hurl lie by the flanks; a ledge rises by a flank. Aliens gather in clusters of varied size and shape (`formations.ts`): pairs, trios, loose crowds, a line across a street, a bow, a ring around something, a file along a road, one big alien with small ones pressed about it, the odd lone sentry. The ground shapes them: on a height, along a wall, about an object, on a road, and a few stand by what the mission is about (the uplink, the terror's heart, the VIP, the commander on the overlook). Reinforcements arrive at set gates behind and beside the aliens.

**Objects.** The hero uses up to three objects a battle in place of an attack: it hurls what stands beside it at an alien, or sets off a fuel tank beside the aliens. It values a tile beside something to hurl.

**Engaging.** Aliens stand in clusters; none patrols. The deploy screen shows every alien on the map, but burrowers, which stay hidden underground. Each battle is plainly one of two flows, by mission and challenge. A push (Clear them out, Takedown, Bring them back, VIP rescue, raids): clusters stand apart along the fight's axis; the one nearest the way in engages, and each other holds its post and fire until the hero comes within 5 tiles or one of it is hurt, then engages whole, so the hero knocks them down one at a time, with several on it at once. If nobody is fighting for two rounds, or six pass in one fight, the nearest cluster comes on its own. A flood (Hold, Hunted, Rescue, a Swarm): a third of the force is posted and fights at once; the rest pours in as clumps of two to five every second round, at a gate clear of the hero. Once the hero is in, fog darkens every tile it does not see. The hero never hides: every turn is a full turn, and it marches on the fight at its full move from the first, closing on the enemy nearest it. It never takes cover or weighs what could shoot it: with a shot it takes the tile with the best shot, counting height and something to hurl. With no shot it dashes on to one if it can, else takes a standard action in place of attacking: with an alien able to reach it next turn it prepares, overwatching to shoot the first alien to come in range; otherwise it sprints, moving again toward the fight or the way out. While any alien is in sight, it avoids stepping where it would draw in an alien waiting its turn, so one cluster falls before the next engages. Walled in, it punches through the walls about it.

**Power curve.** An alien kind's health never changes, so the aliens that frighten a fresh hero are mown down by a built-up one. The aliens come in numbers: a fight going the hero's way looks the same, but one slipping turns deadly fast. Difficulty rises in ways the player can see: the alien force grows with each leg, new kinds appear, and deeper aliens are better equipped, with a point of armor for each step along the road and a point of damage for every third.

**Health and armor.** Every unit shows its health and armor as pips, one each, ten to a row: health in red, health lost in dark, armor in yellow. Armor is temporary health: it takes damage before health does, and is whole again at the start of each fight. It reduces no hit.

**Aliens.** Aliens win by rules no hero has, after XCOM's aliens and StarCraft's Zerg. A mission has a force to spend on themed handfuls of aliens: four swarmlings, two kinhounds with a trooper, a psion with a vise and an aegis, and so on; deeper along the road, more appear. Each then stands alone. As in D&D and superhero films, most aliens fight up close and a few shooters are a nuisance behind them; only a Slog and a Firing line are mostly shooters. Each brawler has one rule: a leaper pounces once over the front onto the hero who shoots farthest, a vise holds the hero it hits through their next turn, a dreadhorn rams harder after a run-up and drives a hero two tiles, an aegis halves every hit but one from beside it, a kinhound hits harder for each alien beside its prey, and a zealot hits harder as it is hurt. Deeper along the road come aliens that each punish a hero lacking one answer and fold to a hero that has it: a juggernaut's plates to many hits or shred, an incubator to burst before it hatches, a wraith to crits, burrowers that surface beside the hero to overwatch, a feeder that grows on every trick to plain shooting, a regenerator to fire, sprinters to chill, a hive node to reach, and a mirror, which hits as hard as the hero, to a hero whose strength is in tricks over raw damage. An Emplacement is a turret, an automated gun that never moves and shoots any hero in its sights, with a vise beside it. The first time the player meets one, the battle pauses on a full screen of it, its name and one calm line on what it will do, until the player acknowledges it. The flight map tells what each stop tests, and how many aliens of each danger, small, medium or large, it holds; a Seer's Uplink relic names them. Before the landing, the player is shown every alien where it stands, and what each kind does.

- Grunt: shoots from range, picking its ground.
- Swarmling: weak, fast, attacks only up close, and comes in numbers.
- Spitter: its spit leaves acid that burns whoever is still in it after their side moves.
- Burster: runs at the hero and explodes, beside it or wherever it dies.
- Brute: tough, attacks only up close, and charges whoever hits it.
- Psion: panics a hero, who loses a turn of action; its death hurts the aliens near it.
- Harvester: on rescues; fast, attacks only up close, and a civilian it kills rises next turn as a husk, a weak alien that attacks up close.
- Boss: guards a satellite or the hub. Each key already won makes the next boss tougher and adds to its escort; from the second satellite on it spawns swarmlings.

**Attacks.** Every attack hits, and every attack that deals damage deals at least 1, so damage is steady and the hero wears down battle by battle. What changes it is height and skills; crits come at their stated chance. Tall objects and walls block shots; nothing shoots through a wall. An alien moves to where its best shot most outweighs the best shot coming back, bolder at full health and more careful as it is wounded. A badly wounded hero fights from out of arm's reach while a healthier ally, its mech, fights on.

**Maps.** Each battlefield is built from objects, after XCOM 2's plots and parcels: roads cut it into lots, and each lot is a place, such as a house with rooms and furniture, a park of trees, a car park, a fenced depot of crates, a warehouse, or an open field. Vehicles stand on the roads. A zone on the road names the kind of battlefield its battles are fought in: town, city, countryside, industrial yard, or ruins. Among their lots are squares with a monument, markets, orchards with lanes between the trees, graveyards, and buildings fallen in. A satellite stands in a walled compound with a gate on each side. The alien source is a hive of spires and growths with nothing human in it. Walls and windows stand on the lines between tiles, with doorways through them; a hero shoots through a window and not through a wall. Trees, trucks and stacks are tall, blocking movement and shots; cars, crates, fences and windows are low, blocking movement alone. A blast wrecks the objects and walls it catches, opening new lines of fire. Roofs within the arena's circle, and over any unit in view, are cut away, so what stands inside shows; the rest stay on. Post-apocalyptic cities and countryside are the setting.

**Run.** A run begins by picking its one hero, a small globe turning to the city they wait in. A run is three legs, each crossing a continent to a city whose satellite holds an access key: the leg ends in its assault, a Guardian's fight. Then a last leg leads to the alien hub; winning that final mission saves the world. The map, Flight, is a globe filling the screen under the header, with no instructions, framed on where the hero stands and the stops ahead, far enough in that no two crowd, and turned so the way on leads up: each stop a step along the way across the continent, always at a real town, the steps uneven and the trails never crossing; the journey so far in gold, trails dotted from where the hero stands to each choice, the choices pulsing. One tap on a stop picks it and lights the way on from it; a second flies the hero's helicopter there, and into a fight dives down through the clouds. The globe glides from view to view, the choices fading in as it settles. A battle's town is built the way its continent builds.

**Road.** A leg is a map of trails that part and meet, nine stops long whichever way is taken, then its satellite. Each stop leads to one, two or three in the next column, and the player picks among them. It shows the stop the hero stands on and the stops still in reach up to three ahead; stops behind and stops beyond sight stay hidden. A leg's first stops are battles; the rest are drawn at random: a battle, a shop, an unknown stop, or a supply drop or gear cache, which need no fight. At every other split one battle becomes a safehouse (🏠), where the hero heals fully instead of fighting. Each battle has its own risk, goal and kind of battlefield: a quiet one has fewer aliens and pays no gear; a contested one is a fair fight with an even chance of a piece of gear; an overrun one has more aliens and pays a piece of rare gear. So a choice weighs different journeys, not easy against hard. A satellite and the alien hub are each guarded by a boss.

**Missions.** Each battle has a goal, and each goal pays its own reward, so the road chosen steers the build. Clear them out: kill every alien, for supplies and gear. Hold and assassinate missions appear from the second leg, so a fresh hero never meets them. Takedown: kill a commander and the rest flee, for rare gear; it is armored, comes with two guards, three deeper along the road, survives any one shot from full health, and blinks to safety once brought to half. Hold the uplink: the uplink stands at the arena's heart and the entry point by it; end six rounds with the hero in its zone while clumps of aliens pour in every second round, at a gate clear of it; then an extraction zone appears in the edge, and they keep coming until the hero has lifted off from it, for a relic; aliens take the uplink, then go for the hero. Rescue: a terror site. Ten civilians are scattered about the terror's heart, and the hero bursts in among them. The aliens come for the hero, but an alien with no good shot at it shoots a civilian instead, a civilian's shot counting for less than the hero's. So a hero that hangs back loses civilians, and one that pushes in, taking the risk, saves more. Civilians flee the aliens toward the hero, and the hero, never cautious here, moves on the alien nearest a civilian, or with none near one, the nearest civilian, taking a chance to reach a civilian over a shot; the hero, or a mech it called down, beside a civilian gets them out. Saving more than half wins it; saving half or fewer loses it, and it pays nothing. Won, each saved pays supplies and the hero experience, and every four left behind add a threat; it ends once none is left. VIP rescue, from the second leg: a VIP is held in a building within, two aliens standing guard. The hero, once within 3 tiles, blows in the wall between them, catching the guards so they do not shoot on their next turn; within 3 tiles it frees them once no alien in the fight that can move stands within 10 tiles. Then an extraction zone appears in the edge, a walk of about 24 tiles away. The VIP follows the hero there, keeping within 2 tiles of it, while the hero keeps by the VIP; nothing hurts a held VIP, but the aliens shoot a freed one before anyone. The VIP lifts off at the zone, handed on by the hero, and that wins it; losing the VIP loses the mission. Won, it pays supplies and a relic. Smash and grab: three guarded caches; each grabbed pays supplies. Hovering a battle on the map counts its aliens by kind.

**Threat.** One bar for the whole invasion, and the run's main race. Every stop adds two, and a lost battle more, so a longer road costs more. The hunt bites as the bar fills: every battle has more aliens, up to half again at full, and the map's force for a stop counts them. When it is full, the hero is ambushed on the road before the next stop: survive the ambush, which empties the bar if won. Unpaid down, it fills about once a leg. As in War of the Chosen, the hero is hunted: there is no landing and no planning; the game cuts straight to the hero in the arena, the Hunter engaged first, a mini-boss built on the hero's own numbers. The hero stands and fights to the last alien, and almost always ends it bruised. Ambushes are meant to be the main way a run is lost.

**Start.** A title screen leads to the run's first choice, as Slay the Spire's opening blessing: three heroes of different classes, each card a compact character sheet; the one picked plays the whole run. Every hero starts with a random class and a random common weapon, melee included.

**Overworld.** Between stops the player moves among three views: the Map, which shows the whole run as trails of stops and the ways on; the Kit, which lists what the hero's build makes, oaths, boons and relics; and the Hero, which shows its health, character sheet and feats. After a stop come the choices it left, one screen each: a relic, each piece of gear found, each skill owed. A skill screen names the hero and its options, and nothing of what lies after. Then one debrief reveals the threat and supplies, and the hero by class badge, name and weapon, its experience bar filling and flaring on a level gained, and feats it pulled off; beneath it a crate for each find. The player then returns to the Map.

**Losing.** A run is lost only in a battle, never on the map, and suddenly rather than slowly. The hero falling in any battle ends the run, so every battle is life or death, as does losing an ambush, a satellite or the hub. A road battle lost with the hero standing adds its threat and a loss's, and pays nothing. Health carries from battle to battle, as in Slay the Spire, and nothing heals on the road. Healing is a choice: at every other split of the way, a safehouse stands beside a battle and heals the hero fully in place of that fight and its rewards, and each shop sells one medkit. So a strong hero rampages, and a hurt one gives up a fight to rest, or risks a fall that ends the run. The map shows the hero's health at every choice of way, as do the Hero view, the debrief and the battle, with its armor.

**Progression.**

- Shops: won battles, supply drops and sold gear pay supplies, spent only at shops on the road: four weapons with an effect, most of the hero's own weapon and two of others, so a build can change hands; four relics; a boon; and a medkit that heals the hero some, once. The hero can never buy one out. Each shop rolls its own stock, and no two pieces of gear in it share an effect. What is bought stays in its place, greyed out and marked Purchased, so the shop never shifts under the player's hand.
- Boons: the run starts with a pick of two of three commons; the hero holds up to three, kept for the run and never used up. Each is common, rare or epic: a combo, rare or epic, does one effect in full and one or two others at less. More are rare finds, the commoner the likelier: from won battles, a satellite, an ambush and shops; finding one with three held forces a choice of which to drop. On the deploy screen the player reads the map and the way in and picks one for that fight, or with none held taps to start; an ambush has no entry point and so no boon. They are swiftness (more move), levitation (no extra cost on steep or difficult ground, over chasms and down drops, and no fall when shoved), overcharge (more damage), sunder (attacks ignore armor), a rigged tank (the player picks a fuel tank; it blows once aliens crowd it, a hero close by caught too), obscuring mist (for three rounds no alien shoots beyond two tiles), a blinding flash (the first cluster stunned on entry), a ranged shield and a melee shield (attacks of that kind do half damage).
- Unknown stops: as Slay the Spire's question marks, revealed on arrival as a battle, a shop, or an event met once a run. An event offers options that each help and hurt. Some swear an oath (`doctrines.ts`) that changes the rules for the rest of the run: Power Armor (armor, for move), Iron Skin (every hit on either side does less), Brawlers (damage for range). Fighting oaths change how the hero fights, one way at a time: Blademasters (melee only, charging in, blunting shots from range), Psi Awakened (attacks through walls, ignoring armor), Wreckers (each hit wrecks the objects around its target), Gunslingers (a third shot a turn). Others trade supplies, threat, levels, health, class and relics.
- Hero: one hero plays the whole run; nobody joins and nobody replaces them. A satellite won pays a relic, a piece of rare gear and a chance of a boon. In the debrief the hero, if owed a skill, has a button to choose it, and each find is a crate to open; both open in a pop-up over the debrief, which stays still behind, and the crate shows what the find would replace and how the hero's stats would change, or sells it. The hero gains experience from every battle, fallen or not: a little for fighting and winning, the rest earned by what it did, its kills and damage done. A feat, such as killing an alien at full health with one hit, pays a little more the first time it is pulled off. Every hero has one of eight classes: the Vanguard, Powerhouse, Deadeye and Beacon; the Gadgeteer, who deploys a combat mech once aliens are in sight, or a swarm of light drones with Drone Swarm; the Pyro, whose Fireball sets a knot of aliens and their ground alight; the Cryo, whose every hit chills; and the Phantom, a superhero who cloaks when aliens have it in their sights: cloaked, no alien sees or targets it, and its next attack strikes out of nowhere, harder and past armor, and reveals it. The cloak is a power, not sneaking: the Phantom fights every turn, and shimmers, faint, on the player's screen. The Pyro and Cryo start a burn or frost build. A mech is a frail decoy, 3 health, that every alien able to shoot it must shoot; it fights beside the hero, and its kills are the hero's, but it cannot hold the field alone. Drones, 2 health each, are free for the aliens to ignore. A class is a set of stat changes; a way of positioning, such as a Deadeye hanging back, which its weapon may override; and one ability, which works with any weapon, the hero uses unprompted. An ability either replaces the hero's shot, as a Powerhouse's Meteor Strike does, or comes on top of it, as a Beacon's Healing Light, which heals the hero or its mech, does.
- Levels and powers: levels never stop; each takes more experience than the last. Each class has a tree of 30 skills, each a guard skill that keeps the hero alive (shrugging off damage, absorbing a hit, armor, decoys, healing), a control skill that holds or moves the aliens (roots, stuns, knockbacks, throws, walls of fire), or a power skill that hits harder. A hero starts knowing its class power, the tree's root, under which stand three paths. At level 3 a hero is offered each path's entry; taking one commits it to that path for the run, and the others lock. At levels 5, 7 and 11 it is offered the three skills of its path's next row, the last row its capstones; it learns one and loses the rest. Later levels add only stats. So two runs of a class differ by path and by each row's choice, and minor perks at the levels between add more: throws, jumps, health, crit, an extra use of the class ability. Many skills are a hero's: a Vanguard leaps into a knot of aliens and throws them, a Powerhouse slams the ground or roars them into a stupor, a Gadgeteer pulses an EMP, a Beacon blinds them with holy light. Skills borrow from tabletop role-playing: a reaction shot on the enemy's turn, bonus damage on an exposed target, auras that help the hero and allies near it, marking a target, getting back up once when felled, a second action once a battle, forcing the enemy to target the tank. A skill that helps allies helps the hero too. Whenever a power, a piece of gear or a relic shapes a shot or acts on a unit, its name shows over that unit, so the player sees each one at work.
- Relics: winning a satellite, a hold, a VIP rescue, an ambush or an Overrun battle offers three relics, and the hero keeps one for the run. A relic changes the hero or how the run pays, as Slay the Spire's do, so each run is built around what it found. Many play on a mechanic: landing relics catch the nearest aliens surprised, blast, strip or chill them where the hero comes in, raise barricades or bring a turret, so they make the way in matter more; Shredder and Cryo Charges are offered only once the hero holds Breaching Charges. Others serve rescues, holds, ambushes and bosses.
- Quantities: burn, chill, marks, moved aliens, impacts, shots, crits, kills, overkill, armor and hits taken are what skills, gear and relics make or read, so pieces made for different builds combine on the one hero: a cryo's chill makes a hammer's knockbacks slide further. Opposites cancel on one alien, burn against chill, a knock against a freeze or a mark, unless the hero holds the rare relic that turns the meeting into a payoff. Cards colour each quantity in their text, lit where the hero makes it, define it and its cancellations in a box on hover, and one relic in three offered reads what the hero makes or makes what it reads. See [combos](combos.md).
- Resists: some aliens shrug off a keyword, so no build is safe everywhere. Bursters cannot burn, brutes cannot be chilled, psions cannot be marked, and spitters' acid ignores armor. Each leg's boss resists one keyword, shown from the start of the leg so the party can build to beat it. 
- One entry point per mission, chosen by the encounter for its flow: a push's head on down the axis, never near an assassination's commander; a flood's by the uplink or among the civilians. The deploy screen shows the whole encounter, its alien kinds counted, and the way in; the player picks a boon or taps to start. An ambush has no entry point.
- Map layouts: each battlefield's plot lies over a shape that decides where the fighting funnels: an open grid of streets; a river crossed only at bridges; a coast that walls one side with water; a blast crater, open and coverless in the middle; or long walls that cut three lanes joined at a few gaps. Water blocks standing but not shots.
- Mission AI: the hero fights to its goal. On a hold it heads for the uplink and stands its ground within 3 tiles of it, and the aliens converge there; on an assassination it closes on the commander once sighted; on a rescue it pushes in, careless of risk, on the alien nearest a civilian; on a VIP rescue it makes for the VIP, then escorts it out. A battle's alien force is its challenge's, by leg and rung, and varies by one either way.
- High ground: hills rise a level, their crests two. A unit sees 2 tiles farther for each level it stands above what it looks at, and 2 less for each below; a shot does 1 more damage per level downhill and 1 less uphill; and a step up a level costs 2 extra move, so charging a hill is slow. Raised tiles are drawn higher and lighter.
- Gear: gear is rare. A won battle drops at most one piece, by its goal and risk; a won satellite and a cache drop one, of the hero's own weapon. Each piece is a weapon built from parts, as Borderlands builds guns: the weapon itself, a stat mod, some of which trade one stat for another, or on rare and epic gear one effect, such as setting targets burning. The hero holds one item, its weapon; aliens keep their armor, and a hero's armor comes only from skills, relics and oaths. Found gear is decided at once: the hero takes it, replacing what it held, or sells it. Nothing is taken off or stored.
- Weapons: a weapon's base is the weapon itself, and any class wields any weapon. Weapons carry a superhero's or a myth's names: the arc caster, star lance that hangs back and strikes from afar, blast gauntlet that closes in, storm darts, storm engine that suppresses, twin blasters, and melee weapons that reach only the next tile and charge in: a thunder hammer that knocks aliens back, a sunblade that cleaves, a star shield that adds armor and blunts attacks from range, and power fists. A weapon's way of positioning, where it has one, overrides its wielder's class's.
- Falling: a hero brought to nothing falls where it stands, and the battle is over. Once a battle Revivify, and once a run Phoenix Protocol, gets it back up first. Fallen in any battle, the run is lost.
