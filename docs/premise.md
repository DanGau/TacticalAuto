# Premise

**Genre.** Isometric tactical auto battler. Its battles are in the style of XCOM; its squad is not. The player owns a small squad of cloned super soldiers who fight on their own, and builds each one up over a run.

**Story.** Aliens have landed at the South Pole; their hub lies under the Antarctic ice. The player gathers an elite squad of the world's last heroes, crossing the globe continent by continent to reach each one, then raids the hub. The aliens hunt the squad harder with every stop it makes. A brief story opens each run, before its first choice.

**Tone.** Science fantasy more than XCOM's grit: cloned super soldiers, powers and alien artifacts are welcome; magic by that name is not.

**Loop.** Roguelike. A won run saves the world; then more aliens arrive and the next run begins.

**Battle.** An irregular patch of a town somewhere in the world, larger than the screen; the camera follows the action. Two borders shape it: inside the inner one stand the pods and objectives; between it and the outer edge the squad comes in and gets out. The player's only decision is which of three landing zones the squad drops into, chosen for its cover and its distance from the aliens. The battle then plays out in alternating turns, humans first. In a turn every unit of the side moves at once, then each shoots, one after another. Unit AI is predictable, so the player can foresee what a landing will do.

**Pods.** As in XCOM: Enemy Unknown, aliens roam in small groups, unseen. Before the landing the map marks where each pod is. Once the squad lands, fog darkens every tile no soldier sees. Stealth runs both ways. A soldier notices an unseen alien within 8 tiles, a patrolling alien a soldier within 6; cover and holding still shrink that reach, and high ground stretches it. Unseen, soldiers creep at a little over half their move. Until the fight starts both sides move at once, without turns. A pod the squad notices first goes on unaware while the squad holds its fire and sets up: unnoticed, with a shot, from cover. Once every soldier has a shot, or after two turns, the squad springs the ambush together, its shots that turn hitting and critting more often. If the pod notices the squad first, the fight is on with no ambush. A pod that notices the squad first stalks it unseen and opens fire with the same bonus, giving itself away. When both notice at once the pod scrambles to cover. Once every alert alien is dead the squad is unseen again, free to sneak on the next pod. By default the squad walks in quietly from the edge. With the Orbital Drop Pods relic one landing zone is a hot drop onto a pod: the drop pods hurt every alien beside the landing, and that pod is ambushed and cannot shoot its first turn, but every other pod hears the landing and converges on it. With Night Canopies one zone is a parachute drop anywhere clear of the pods. The squad moves as one: every soldier closes on the same enemy, the one nearest the squad's centre, and stays near that centre. A landing therefore decides which pod the squad meets first and on what ground. A soldier with a shot takes the safest cover it can shoot from, then the best shot from there. A soldier with no shot advances from cover to cover, never stopping in the open when cover a step nearer will do. While any alien is in sight, soldiers avoid stepping where they would uncover another pod, so one fight ends before the next begins. On the march, Snipers keep behind the rest of the squad.

**Power curve.** An alien kind's health and aim never change, so the aliens that are frightening to one clone are mown down by four built-up ones. Difficulty rises in ways the player can see: the alien force grows with each leg, new packs appear, and deeper aliens are better equipped, with a point of armor for each step along the road and a point of damage for every third.

**Health and armor.** Every unit shows its health and armor as pips, one each, ten to a row: health in red, health lost in dark, armor in yellow. Armor takes damage before health does.

**Aliens.** Aliens win by rules no soldier has, after XCOM's aliens and StarCraft's Zerg. Aliens come in themed packs, each a pod: a Swarm of swarmlings, an Acid team of spitters with a trooper, a Psi cell of a psion and its guards, and so on. A mission has a force to spend on packs; deeper along the road, more packs appear. Deeper along the road come aliens that each punish a squad lacking one answer and fold to a squad that has it: a juggernaut's plates to many hits or shred, an incubator to burst before it hatches, a wraith to crits, burrowers that surface beside the loneliest soldier to overwatch, a feeder that grows on every trick to plain shooting, a regenerator to fire, sprinters to chill, a hive node to reach, and a mirror, which hits as hard as the squad's hardest hitter, to a squad whose strength is spread. The first time the player meets one, the battle pauses on a full screen of it, its name and one calm line on what it will do, until the player acknowledges it. The map tells only how many aliens of each danger, small, medium or large, a zone holds; a Recon Uplink relic names them. Before landing, the player is shown each pod by name and where it is, its members, and what each kind does; a pod with a kind the player has not met is an unknown contact.

- Trooper: shoots from cover, as a soldier does.
- Swarmling: weak, fast, attacks only up close, and comes in numbers.
- Spitter: its spit never misses, and leaves acid that burns whoever is still in it after their side moves.
- Burster: runs at the squad and explodes, beside a soldier or wherever it dies.
- Brute: tough, attacks only up close, and charges whoever hits it.
- Psion: panics a soldier, who loses a turn of action; its death hurts its pod.
- Boss: guards an extraction or the hub. Each hero already extracted makes the next boss tougher and adds a trooper to its escort; from the second extraction on it spawns swarmlings.

**Cover.** Cover is real objects and follows reality: nothing shoots through a wall. High cover blocks movement and shots; a unit behind it can be hit only from an angle that sees past it. Low cover blocks movement and makes a unit beside it harder to hit. A unit takes cover from what stands on an edge of its tile, or on the tile beyond that edge. Cover counts only against a shooter on its far side; a shot from any other side flanks, ignores the cover, and can crit. Hit and crit numbers follow XCOM: Enemy Unknown. A shot is likelier to hit the closer the target, so a flanked enemy at point-blank is a near-certain hit. By default a unit moves to where its best shot most outweighs the best shot coming back. Every unit is bolder at full health and more careful as it is wounded; a badly wounded soldier pulls back while a healthier squadmate fights on.

**Maps.** Each battlefield is built from objects, after XCOM 2's plots and parcels: roads cut it into lots, and each lot is a place, such as a house with rooms and furniture, a park of trees, a car park, a fenced depot of crates, a warehouse, or an open field. Vehicles stand on the roads. A zone on the road names the kind of battlefield its battles are fought in: town, city, countryside, industrial yard, or ruins. Among their lots are squares with a monument, markets, orchards with lanes between the trees, graveyards, and buildings fallen in. A satellite stands in a walled compound with a gate on each side. The alien source is a hive of spires and growths with nothing human in it. Walls and windows stand on the lines between tiles, with doorways through them; a soldier shoots through a window and not through a wall. Trees, trucks and stacks are high cover; cars, crates, fences and windows are low. A rocket or grenade wrecks the objects and walls it catches, opening new lines of fire. Post-apocalyptic cities and countryside are the setting.

**Run.** A run begins by picking the soldier who leads the squad, a small globe turning to the city they wait in. A run is three legs, each crossing a continent to a city where an elite hero holds out: the leg ends in an extraction, a guarded fight that adds the hero to the squad, seasoned. Then a last leg leads to the alien hub; winning that final mission saves the world. The map, Flight, is a globe filling the screen under the header, with no instructions, framed on where the squad stands and the stops ahead, far enough in that no two crowd, and turned so the way on leads up: each stop a step along the way across the continent, at a real town where one lies near, the steps uneven and the trails never crossing; the journey so far in gold, trails dotted from where the squad stands to each choice, the choices pulsing. One tap on a stop picks it and lights the way on from it; a second flies the squad's helicopter there, and into a fight dives down through the clouds. The globe glides from view to view, the choices fading in as it settles. A battle's town is built the way its continent builds.

**Road.** A leg is a map of trails that part and meet, nine stops long whichever way is taken, then its extraction. Each stop leads to one, two or three in the next column, and the player picks among them. It shows the stop the squad stands on and the stops still in reach up to three ahead; stops behind and stops beyond sight stay hidden. A leg's first stops are battles; the rest are drawn at random: a battle, a shop, an unknown stop, or a supply drop or gear cache, which need no fight. Each battle has its own risk, goal and kind of battlefield: a quiet one has fewer aliens and pays no gear; a contested one is a fair fight; an overrun one has more aliens and pays more, in rare gear. So a choice weighs different journeys, not easy against hard. An extraction and the alien hub are each guarded by a boss.

**Missions.** Each battle has a goal, and each goal pays its own reward, so the road chosen steers the build. Exterminate: kill every alien, for supplies and gear. Assassinate: kill a tough commander and the rest flee, for rare gear. Hold the uplink: the uplink stands within, and the squad comes at it from the edge; reach it and end six rounds with a soldier in its zone while waves arrive, for a relic; aliens take the uplink, then go for the squad. Rescue: ten civilians hide about the map. Aliens hunt the ones found, and soldiers make for them, taking a chance to reach one over a shot; a soldier beside a civilian gets them out. Each saved pays supplies and every soldier standing experience, and every four left behind add a threat; it ends once none is left. A rescue has no landing zones: the one choice is whether the squad lands together, in two teams, or each soldier alone, every team landing apart and searching on its own. Supply raid: guarded caches; each grabbed pays supplies, and each sought beyond the first adds a threat. Hovering a battle on the map counts its aliens by kind.

**Threat.** One bar for the whole invasion. Every stop adds to it, and a lost battle adds more, so a longer road costs more. When it is full, the squad is ambushed on the road before the next stop: an escape, which empties the bar if won. The bar is long, so escapes are rare. As in War of the Chosen, the squad is hunted: it starts in one corner, a Hunter tougher, faster and deadlier than any trooper runs it down, and the packs come again as waves. There is no landing; the player picks one of three escape zones across the map, at the far corner or the edges beside it, seeing the aliens across each way out, and any soldier who reaches it lifts off. Each soldier downed costs supplies. Escapes are meant to be the main way a run is lost.

**Start.** A title screen leads to the run's first choice, as Slay the Spire's opening blessing: three recruits of different classes, each card showing its class, ability, path and trait. One is plain; each other has a trait, a strength paid for with a weakness, such as joining at level 3 for threat, or more aim for less health.

**Overworld.** Between stops the player moves among three views: the Map, which shows the whole run as trails of stops and the ways on; the Base, which lists the squad's keywords, doctrines and relics; and the Barracks, which shows each soldier, its path, skills and feats. After a stop come the choices it left, one screen each: a relic, each piece of gear found, each skill owed. A skill screen names the soldier and its two options, and nothing of what lies after. Then one summary reveals the threat, supplies, and each soldier's experience and promotion, with quiet notes on gear it decided on and feats it pulled off that tell more on hover. The player then returns to the Map.

**Losing.** A run is lost only in a battle, never on the map, and suddenly rather than slowly. Losing an escape, an extraction or the final mission ends the run. Losing any other battle adds threat and earns nothing. Soldiers heal fully between battles.

**Progression.**

- Shops: won battles, supply drops and sold gear pay supplies, spent only at shops on the road: gear with an effect, fitted to the soldier it suits; relics; or a bribe that lowers the threat. A relic can instead be taken on credit, for threat. Each shop rolls its own stock, and no two pieces of gear in it share an effect.
- Unknown stops: as Slay the Spire's question marks, revealed on arrival as a battle, a shop, or an event met once a run. An event offers options that each help and hurt. Some adopt a doctrine that changes the rules for the rest of the run: Mass Cloning (two more clones, less health), Lone Wolf (one soldier, much stronger, no recruits), Specialists (every soldier a different class), Exosuits (armor, but no defense from cover), Fortification (cover counts for more on both sides), Scatterguns (damage for range). Weapon doctrines change how the whole squad fights, and the squad fights one way at a time: Blademasters (melee only, charging in, parrying shots from range), Psi Operatives (attacks through walls, ignoring cover), Demolitions (each hit wrecks the cover around its target), Gunslingers (two shots a turn). Others trade supplies, threat, clones and relics.
- Squad: the run begins with one soldier. Each extraction won adds a hero, to four for the final mission; a hero joins at level 2, its skills learned. A soldier who falls in battle is cloned anew, with level, class, skills and gear intact, so the player keeps investing in the same few soldiers. Only soldiers still standing at a battle's end gain experience: a little shared for surviving and winning, the rest earned by what each did, its kills and damage done, so a soldier who carries a fight snowballs. A feat, such as killing an alien at full health with one hit, pays a little more the first time a soldier pulls it off, so later recruits can catch up by doing what the veteran already has. Every clone has one of five classes, drawn at random: XCOM's Assault, Heavy, Sniper and Support, and the Engineer, who deploys a combat mech once aliens are in sight, or a swarm of light drones on the Dronemaster path. A mech fights for the squad, and its kills are its engineer's, but it cannot hold the field alone. A class is a set of stat changes; a way of positioning, such as a Sniper keeping aliens at its rifle's full range; and one ability the soldier uses unprompted. An ability either replaces the soldier's shot, as a Heavy's rocket does, or comes on top of it, as a Support's heal does.
- Levels and skills: levels never stop; each takes more experience than the last. Each class has two paths, such as the Assault's Berserker and Infiltrator. A clone arrives knowing the core skill of one, drawn at random; at each of its next two levels it learns one of a pair along that path, and the other is lost. Later levels add only stats. So two soldiers of a class differ from run to run. Skills borrow from tabletop role-playing: a reaction shot on the enemy's turn, rolling twice to hit, bonus damage on an exposed target, auras that help squadmates nearby, marking a target, getting a fallen squadmate up, a second action once a battle, forcing the enemy to target the tank. Whenever a skill, a piece of gear or a relic shapes a shot or acts on a unit, its name shows over that unit, so the player sees each one at work.
- Relics: winning an extraction, a hold, an escape or an Overrun battle offers three relics, and the squad keeps one for the run. A relic changes every soldier or how the run pays, as Slay the Spire's do, so each run is built around what it found. Many play on a mechanic: drop pod relics hurt, strip or chill the aliens where the squad lands, wall it in or drop a turret, so they make where it lands matter more; others serve stealth and ambushes, rescues, holds, escapes and bosses.
- Keywords: Burn, Frost, Mark, Crit, Kill and Ward tie skills, gear and relics together, as a deckbuilder's card types do. Some pieces cause a keyword, such as gear that chills or a skill that marks; others pay off on it, such as a relic that adds damage to chilled targets. Two keywords can meet: a crit that sets a target burning, a hit on a target both burning and chilled. Cards colour each keyword in their text, lit where the squad already has one, and define it in a box on hover, and the first relic offered builds on one the squad has.
- Resists: some aliens shrug off a keyword, so no build is safe everywhere. Bursters cannot burn, brutes cannot be chilled, psions cannot be marked, and spitters' acid ignores armor. Each leg's boss resists one keyword, shown from the start of the leg so the squad can build to beat it. 
- One landing decision per mission, on the ground the map gives: an exterminate offers quiet approaches from the edge, plus a hot drop or a parachute drop if a relic allows; an assassination a decapitation, an overwatch on high ground at range, or bait that draws the guards off; a hold which of three uplinks to hold; a raid how many of its guarded caches to go for; a rescue how to split the squad; an escape which way out. Zones clear of the aliens differ in kind, high ground, heavy cover or close, and which suits depends on the squad; the game does not say. Before any landing the squad can call an orbital strike that wounds every alien, for threat.
- Map layouts: each battlefield's plot lies over a shape that decides where the fighting funnels: an open grid of streets; a river crossed only at bridges; a coast that walls one side with water; a blast crater, open and coverless in the middle; or long walls that cut three lanes joined at a few gaps. Water blocks standing but not shots, and its bank is low cover.
- Mission AI: the squad fights to its goal. On a hold it heads for the uplink and stands its ground within 3 tiles of it, and the aliens converge there; on an assassination it closes on the commander once sighted; on a rescue it engages the aliens nearest the civilians. Each battle's alien force varies by one either way.
- High ground: hills rise a level, their crests two. A unit sees 2 tiles farther for each level it stands above what it looks at, and 2 less for each below; a shot gains 10% aim per level downhill and loses it uphill; and a step up a level costs 2 extra move, so charging a hill is slow. Raised tiles are drawn higher and lighter.
- Gear: won battles and caches drop gear, each found by a soldier drawn at random. Each is built from parts, as Borderlands builds guns: a base, one or two stat mods, some of which trade one stat for another, and on rare and epic gear one effect, such as setting targets burning or absorbing the first hit. A soldier has three slots: weapon, armor, utility. Found gear is decided at once, beside what the finder wears and how its stats would change: equip it on the finder, selling what it replaces, or sell it. Nothing is stored. Gear survives a death.
- Death: should reward the player in a way the fiction supports. Undecided.
