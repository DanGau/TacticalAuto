# Premise

**Genre.** Isometric superhero deck-builder, in the spirit of Slay the Spire, on a tactical board, against an alien invasion. The player picks one hero, who fights on their own, kicking down the door, and builds them up over a run. One hero, so the player always knows who is doing what, and each hero has room for depth.

**Story.** Aliens have landed at the South Pole; their hub lies under the Antarctic ice. The player's hero crosses the globe continent by continent, taking a satellite on each for its access key, then raids the hub. The aliens hunt the hero harder with every stop it makes. A brief story opens each run, before its first choice.

**Theme.** Superheroes on Dungeons & Dragons rules, against an alien invasion. Powers over military tech: a sorcerer fights beside a tech genius, though it is not full fantasy, and the aliens stay aliens. The game speaks of the hero, HP, Block, Energy and cards. A hero reads as a character sheet: name, class and level, HP, armor, move, range, weapon and deck (`ui/sheet.ts`); an alien as a stat block, on the deploy screen when tapped and on first meeting.

**Loop.** Roguelike. A won run saves the world; then more aliens arrive and the next run begins.

**Battle.** A town somewhere in the world, 96 tiles square, many screens across. The camera frames what is in play and follows the action, a drag pans it, and it shows past the map only to keep the hero in frame. The hero lands at one end of an arena about 30 tiles across, A, and its goal waits at the other, B, about 26 tiles on: an extraction zone, an uplink, civilians, a commander or a Guardian. The arena is fought over its ground (`arena-kinds.ts`), which the deploy screen names and marks where its feature lies, beside every alien where it stands. Nothing strikes the whole battlefield. The hero never runs: every fight is the hero against the aliens near it. The battle is played in rounds, waiting on the player each turn ([cards](cards.md)): the aliens move and the hero moves on its own; the player plays cards from a hand drawn from the deck, spending Energy, to attack, Block, move and build; then each alien acts on the intent shown over it. An Attack needs a target in range and line of sight, so a card that moves the hero or an alien has real value. Unit AI is predictable, so the player can foresee what a card will do.

**Challenges.** Each battle tests one thing, readable at a glance on the Flight map and the deploy screen (`challenges.ts`), through its aliens, ground and clock: Slog (a swamp of mire with small groups scattered across it), Firing line (long-range shooters on a ridge), Iron wall (heavily armored aliens), Swarm (bodies flood in), Scattered (sentries spread out on a clock, then reinforcements), Big one (a huge armored boss with an escort), Kill box (a packed cluster at the way in). Tiers by leg draw them, rising up a leg. The first leg has none: its battles are normal fights, the first five stops all battles, so the hero builds a kit before the first test. Meeting one with a deck that has no answer to it costs dear in health and may lose the fight; health carries over, so repeated mismatches end the run. Extractions and the hub are Big ones. So a deck weighs breadth, a card for every test, against leanness, its best cards drawn often.

**Encounters.** Each battle is designed as one set-piece, as a DM prepares an encounter (`encounter.ts`): the fight runs along an axis from the way in to the objective, over the ground its region draws. The arena is cleared, then staged about landmarks (`pois.ts`), each named in the ground's own vocabulary and framed for the camera: the objective, alien nests marked with creep, a hazard with its fuel tanks (the ground's own drop, water, mire or ice where it has one), a vantage (its high ground, else a knoll), and a strongpoint of cover open toward the landing zone. Lanes join the landing, the strongpoint, the vantage and the objective; the ground between stays quiet. Aliens gather in distinct clusters (`formations.ts`), one at each landmark, the strongpoint, the hazard and the vantage, each a nest named for it; a challenge that stages its aliens, a ridge line, lone sentries or a kill box, places its own. Clusters vary in size and shape: pairs, trios, loose crowds, a line across a street, a bow, a ring around something, a file along a road, one big alien with small ones pressed about it, the odd lone sentry. The ground shapes them: on a height, along a wall, about an object, on a road, and a few stand at the objective (the extraction zone, the uplink, the civilians, the commander or the Guardian). Reinforcements arrive at set gates behind and beside the aliens.

**Objects.** A fuel tank goes up when an alien beside it is hit, burning everyone beside it.

**Engaging.** Aliens stand in clusters; none patrols. The deploy screen shows every alien on the map, but burrowers, which stay hidden underground. Each battle is plainly one of two flows, by mission and challenge. A push (Break Through, Takedown, satellites and the hub): clusters stand apart between the way in and the objective; the one nearest the way in engages, and each other holds its post and fire until the hero comes within 5 tiles or one of it is hurt, then engages whole, so the hero knocks them down one at a time, with several on it at once. If nobody is fighting for two rounds, or six pass in one fight, the nearest cluster comes on its own. A flood (Hold, Hunted, Rescue, a Swarm): a third of the force is posted and fights at once; the rest pours in as clumps of two to five every second round, at a gate clear of the hero. Once the hero is in, fog darkens every tile it does not see. The hero's move is automatic and never hides: with no alien to shoot it marches on its goal at its full move, by the cheapest walk: mire and climbs cost it more, and Hover floats it over drops and water. With an alien in the fight it takes the tile with the best shot for its weapon, counting height and an alien it would knock off a ledge or into a drop. While any alien is in sight, it avoids stepping where it would draw in an alien waiting its turn, so one cluster falls before the next engages. It never attacks on its own: every attack is a card.

**Power curve.** An alien kind's health never changes, so the aliens that frighten a fresh hero are mown down by a built-up one. The aliens come in numbers: a fight going the hero's way looks the same, but one slipping turns deadly fast. Difficulty rises in ways the player can see: the alien force grows with each leg, new kinds appear, and deeper aliens are better equipped, with a point of armor for each step along the road and a point of damage for every third. From the third leg, every second alien of a road battle hits for 1 more, so the road wears the hero down between rests.

**Health and armor.** Every unit shows its health and armor as pips, one each, ten to a row: health in red, health lost in dark, armor in yellow. Armor is temporary health: it takes damage before health does, and is whole again at the start of each fight. It reduces no hit.

**Aliens.** Aliens win by rules no hero has, after XCOM's aliens and StarCraft's Zerg. A mission has a force to spend on themed handfuls of aliens: four swarmlings, two kinhounds with a trooper, a psion with a vise and an aegis, and so on; deeper along the road, more appear. Each then stands alone. As in D&D and superhero films, most aliens fight up close and a few shooters are a nuisance behind them; only a Slog and a Firing line are mostly shooters. Each brawler has one rule: a leaper pounces once over the front onto the hero who shoots farthest, a vise holds the hero it hits through their next turn, a dreadhorn rams harder after a run-up and drives a hero two tiles, an aegis halves every hit but one from beside it, a kinhound hits harder for each alien beside its prey, and a zealot hits harder as it is hurt. Deeper along the road come aliens that each punish a hero lacking one answer and fold to a hero that has it: a juggernaut's plates to many hits or shred, an incubator to burst before it hatches, a wraith to crits, burrowers that surface beside the hero to overwatch, a feeder that grows on every trick to plain shooting, a regenerator to fire, sprinters to chill, a hive node to reach, and a mirror, which hits as hard as the hero, to a hero whose strength is in tricks over raw damage. An Emplacement is a turret, an automated gun that never moves and shoots any hero in its sights, with a vise beside it. The first time the player meets one, the battle pauses on a full screen of it, its name and one calm line on what it will do, until the player acknowledges it. The flight map tells what each stop tests, and how many aliens of each danger, small, medium or large, it holds; a Seer's Uplink relic names them. Before the landing, the player is shown every alien where it stands, and what each kind does.

- Grunt: shoots from range, picking its ground.
- Swarmling: weak, fast, attacks only up close, and comes in numbers.
- Spitter: its spit leaves acid that burns whoever is still in it after their side moves.
- Burster: runs at the hero and explodes, beside it or wherever it dies.
- Brute: tough, attacks only up close, and charges whoever hits it.
- Psion: panics a hero, who loses a turn of action; its death hurts the aliens near it.
- Harvester: on rescues; fast, attacks only up close, and a civilian it kills rises next turn as a husk, a weak alien that attacks up close.
- Boss: guards a satellite or the hub. Each key already won makes the next boss tougher and adds to its escort; from the second satellite on it spawns swarmlings.

**Attacks.** Every attack hits, and every attack that deals damage deals at least 1, so damage is steady and the hero wears down battle by battle. What changes it is height, Block and Powers; crits come at their stated chance. Tall objects and walls block shots; nothing shoots through a wall. An alien moves to where its best shot most outweighs the best shot coming back, bolder at full health and more careful as it is wounded.

**Maps.** Each battlefield is built from objects, after XCOM 2's plots and parcels: roads cut it into lots, and each lot is a place, such as a house with rooms and furniture, a park of trees, a car park, a fenced depot of crates, a warehouse, or an open field. Vehicles stand on the roads. A zone on the road names the kind of battlefield its battles are fought in: town, city, countryside, industrial yard, or ruins. Among their lots are squares with a monument, markets, orchards with lanes between the trees, graveyards, and buildings fallen in. A satellite stands in a walled compound with a gate on each side. The alien source is a hive of spires and growths with nothing human in it. Walls and windows stand on the lines between tiles, with doorways through them; a hero shoots through a window and not through a wall. Trees, trucks and stacks are tall, blocking movement and shots; cars, crates, fences and windows are low, blocking movement alone. A blast wrecks the objects and walls it catches, opening new lines of fire. Roofs within the arena's circle, and over any unit in view, are cut away, so what stands inside shows; the rest stay on. Post-apocalyptic cities and countryside are the setting. Each region has its own ground, its signature drawn as often as the rest together (`regions.ts`): a skyline's rooftops, a freeway's overpass, a mining town's quarry and rift, a canal city flooded, a rainy suburb's marsh, a hillside's mesa, an arctic ice sheet. The Flight map shows each stop's ground beside its challenge.

**Run.** A run begins by picking its one hero, a small globe turning to the city they wait in. A run is three legs, each crossing a continent to a city whose satellite holds an access key: the leg ends in its assault, a Guardian's fight. Then a last leg leads to the alien hub; winning that final mission saves the world. The map, Flight, is a globe filling the screen under the header, with no instructions, framed on where the hero stands and the stops ahead, far enough in that no two crowd, and turned so the way on leads up: each stop a step along the way across the continent, always at a real town, the steps uneven and the trails never crossing; the journey so far in gold, trails dotted from where the hero stands to each choice, the choices pulsing. One tap on a stop picks it and lights the way on from it; a second flies the hero's helicopter there, and into a fight dives down through the clouds. The globe glides from view to view, the choices fading in as it settles. A battle's town is built the way its continent builds.

**Road.** A leg is a map of trails that part and meet, nine stops long whichever way is taken, then its satellite. Each stop leads to one, two or three in the next column, and the player picks among them. It shows the stop the hero stands on and the stops still in reach up to three ahead; stops behind and stops beyond sight stay hidden. A leg's first stops are battles; the rest are drawn at random: a battle, a shop, an unknown stop, or a supply drop or gear cache, which need no fight. The last stop before the satellite is a safehouse (🏠) on every trail, where the hero heals fully. Each battle has its own risk, goal and kind of battlefield: a quiet one has fewer aliens and pays no gear; a contested one is a fair fight with an even chance of a piece of gear; an overrun one has more aliens and pays a piece of rare gear. So a choice weighs different journeys, not easy against hard. A satellite and the alien hub are each guarded by a boss.

**Missions.** Each battle has a goal at its objective, and each goal pays its own reward, so the road chosen steers the build. Any battle is won by killing every alien. Break Through: reach the extraction zone at B, or kill every alien, for supplies and gear. Hold and assassinate missions appear from the second leg, so a fresh hero never meets them. Takedown: kill the commander at B and the rest flee, for rare gear; it is armored, comes with two guards, three deeper along the road, survives any one shot from full health, and blinks to safety once brought to half. Hold the uplink: the uplink stands at B; end six rounds with the hero in its zone while clumps of aliens pour in every second round, at a gate clear of it, for a relic; aliens take the uplink, then go for the hero. Rescue: a terror site. Three civilians huddle at B, staying put, and the hero fights its way in to them. The aliens come for the hero, but an alien with no good shot at it shoots a civilian instead, a civilian's shot counting for less than the hero's. So a hero that hangs back loses civilians, and one that pushes in, taking the risk, saves more. The hero, never cautious here, moves on the alien nearest a civilian, or with none near one, the nearest civilian, taking a chance to reach a civilian over a shot; the hero beside a civilian gets them out. Saving two wins it; saving fewer loses it, and it pays nothing. Won, each saved pays supplies and the hero experience, and every two left behind add a threat; it ends once none is left. A satellite's Guardian and the hub's boss wait at B; felled, the rest flee. Hunted, an ambush, is won at the extraction zone or by killing the Hunter. Hovering a battle on the map counts its aliens by kind.

**Threat.** One bar for the whole invasion, and the run's main race. Every stop adds two, and a lost battle more, so a longer road costs more. The hunt bites as the bar fills: every battle has more aliens, up to half again at full, and the map's force for a stop counts them. When it is full, the hero is ambushed on the road before the next stop: survive the ambush, which empties the bar if won. Unpaid down, it fills about once a leg. As in War of the Chosen, the hero is hunted: there is no planning and no boon; the game cuts straight to the hero at A, the Hunter engaged first, a mini-boss built on the hero's own numbers, between it and the extraction zone at B. The hero almost always ends it bruised. Ambushes are meant to be the main way a run is lost.

**Start.** A title screen leads to the run's first choice, as Slay the Spire's opening blessing: three heroes of different classes, each card a compact character sheet; the one picked plays the whole run. Every hero starts with a random class and a random common weapon, melee included, and a starter deck of 10 cards from both ([cards](cards.md)).

**Overworld.** Between stops the player moves among four views: the Map, which shows the whole run as trails of stops and the ways on; the Deck, every card held; the Kit, which lists what the hero's build makes, oaths and relics; and the Hero, which shows its health, character sheet and feats. After a stop come the choices it left, one screen each: a relic, each piece of gear found, the cards offered, a safehouse's rest or upgrade. Then one debrief reveals the threat and supplies, and the hero by class badge, name and weapon, its experience bar filling and flaring on a level gained, and feats it pulled off; beneath it a crate for each find. The player then returns to the Map.

**Losing.** A run is lost only in a battle, never on the map: mostly by a hero worn down over a leg, suddenly only at a boss or elite. The hero falling in any battle ends the run, so every battle is life or death, as does losing an ambush, a satellite or the hub. A road battle lost with the hero standing adds its threat and a loss's, and pays nothing. Health carries from battle to battle, as in Slay the Spire, and nothing heals on the road. Every trail rests at a safehouse just before the satellite, where the hero heals fully or upgrades a card, and each shop sells one medkit. Between rests health is the run's clock. The map shows the hero's health at every choice of way, as do the Hero view, the debrief and the battle, with its armor.

**Progression.**

- Shops: won battles, supply drops and sold gear pay supplies, spent only at shops on the road: four weapons with an effect, most of the hero's own weapon and two of others, so a build can change hands; four relics; three cards; removing a card from the deck, once; and a medkit that heals the hero some, once. The hero can never buy one out. Each shop rolls its own stock, and no two pieces of gear in it share an effect. What is bought stays in its place, greyed out and marked Purchased, so the shop never shifts under the player's hand.
- Cards: every action in battle is a card ([cards](cards.md)). Every battle won offers 3 cards of the hero's class or none, to add 1 or Skip; rarer cards come more often as the run goes on. A safehouse rests or upgrades one. Powers lend gear and relic effects for a battle, so they make or read the combo quantities: [combos](combos.md#cards).
- Unknown stops: as Slay the Spire's question marks, revealed on arrival as a battle, a shop, or an event met once a run. An event offers options that each help and hurt. Some adopt a doctrine (`doctrines.ts`) that leans card offers to a style for the rest of the run: [cards](cards.md). Others trade supplies, threat, max HP, health, class and relics.
- Hero: one hero plays the whole run; nobody joins and nobody replaces them. A satellite won pays a relic, a piece of rare gear and an offer of rare cards. In the debrief each find is a crate to open in a pop-up over the debrief, which stays still behind; the crate shows what the find would replace and how the hero's stats would change, or sells it. Every hero has one of eight classes: the Vanguard, Powerhouse, Deadeye and Beacon, the Gadgeteer, Pyro, Cryo and Phantom. A class is a way of moving and positioning, such as a Deadeye hanging back, which its weapon may override, a signature card in the starter deck, and its own cards among those offered.
- Max HP: there are no levels; max HP comes only from relics and events.
- Relics: winning a satellite, a hold, an ambush or an Overrun battle offers three relics, and the hero keeps one for the run. A relic changes the hero or how the run pays, as Slay the Spire's do, so each run is built around what it found. Many play on a mechanic: landing relics catch the nearest aliens surprised, blast, strip or chill them where the hero comes in, or raise barricades, so they make the way in matter more; Shredder and Cryo Charges are offered only once the hero holds Breaching Charges. Others serve rescues, holds, ambushes and bosses.
- Quantities: burn, chill, marks, moved aliens, impacts, shots, crits, kills, overkill, armor and hits taken are what cards, gear and relics make or read, so pieces made for different builds combine on the one hero: a cryo's chill makes a hammer's knockbacks slide further. Opposites cancel on one alien, burn against chill, a knock against a freeze or a mark, unless the hero holds the rare relic that turns the meeting into a payoff. Cards colour each quantity in their text, lit where the hero makes it, define it and its cancellations in a box on hover, and one relic in three offered reads what the hero makes or makes what it reads. See [combos](combos.md).
- Resists: some aliens shrug off a keyword, so no build is safe everywhere. Bursters cannot burn, brutes cannot be chilled, psions cannot be marked, and spitters' acid ignores armor. Each leg's boss resists one keyword, shown from the start of the leg so the party can build to beat it. 
- One entry point per mission, A, at the far end of the arena from B. The deploy screen shows the ground and its feature, its alien kinds counted, the hand of cards, each with what it plays to on this board, and the way in.
- Map layouts: each battlefield's plot lies over a shape that decides where the fighting funnels: an open grid of streets; a river crossed only at bridges; a coast that walls one side with water; a blast crater, open and coverless in the middle; or long walls that cut three lanes joined at a few gaps. Water blocks standing but not shots.
- Mission AI: the hero's move heads for its goal while it has no shot. Breaking through, that is the extraction zone; on a hold it stands its ground within 3 tiles of the uplink, and the aliens converge there; on a takedown or at a Guardian it fights through to its target; on a rescue it pushes in, careless of risk, on the alien nearest a civilian. A battle's alien force is its challenge's, by leg and rung, and varies by one either way.
- High ground: hills rise a level, their crests two. A unit sees 2 tiles farther for each level it stands above what it looks at, and 2 less for each below; a shot does 1 more damage per level downhill and 1 less uphill; and a step up a level costs 2 extra move, so charging a hill is slow. Raised tiles are drawn higher and lighter.
- Gear: gear is rare. A won battle drops at most one piece, by its goal and risk; a won satellite and a cache drop one, of the hero's own weapon. Each piece is a weapon built from parts, as Borderlands builds guns: the weapon itself, a stat mod, some of which trade one stat for another, or on rare and epic gear one effect, such as setting targets burning. The hero holds one item, its weapon; aliens keep their armor, and a hero's armor comes only from skills, relics and oaths. Found gear is decided at once: the hero takes it, replacing what it held, or sells it. Nothing is taken off or stored.
- Weapons: a weapon's base is the weapon itself, and any class wields any weapon. Weapons carry a superhero's or a myth's names: the arc caster, star lance that hangs back and strikes from afar, blast gauntlet that closes in, storm darts, storm engine that suppresses, twin blasters, and melee weapons that reach only the next tile and charge in: a thunder hammer that knocks aliens back, a sunblade that cleaves, a star shield that adds armor and blunts attacks from range, and power fists. A weapon's way of positioning, where it has one, overrides its wielder's class's.
- Falling: a hero brought to nothing falls where it stands, and the battle is over. Once a battle, Phoenix Protocol gets it back up first. Fallen in any battle, the run is lost.
